Heavy compute and HPC systems are designed for batch-oriented, parallel workloads such as render farms, machine learning training clusters, and genomics processing. They typically serve internal or B2B users with relatively low concurrency, usually a few hundred active workers, but very high per-job resource usage. These workloads involve large file I/O with bursty reads of multi-gigabyte assets and continuous writes of progress or checkpoints. Such systems rely on high-bandwidth networks and parallel file systems like Lustre or GPFS. Tasks often run for tens of minutes and are orchestrated through job queues and schedulers, where throughput and correctness are far more important than low latency.

Real-time interactive low-latency systems power applications like online multiplayer games, collaborative tools, and live chat platforms. They must support extremely high concurrency, often tens or hundreds of thousands of concurrent users, while remaining highly sensitive to latency and jitter. Communication is bi-directional and stateful, with frequent small messages rather than large payloads. These systems typically target end-to-end latencies under 50 milliseconds and rely on technologies such as WebSockets or gRPC, in-memory databases, and pub-sub messaging. State is usually managed by an authoritative server and often deployed close to users using edge computing to reduce network delays.

Content delivery and streaming systems are built to serve massive, mostly read-only audiences for platforms like video-on-demand or podcast streaming. They may serve tens of millions of users and exhibit extremely read-heavy access patterns, with writes occurring primarily during content ingestion or metadata updates. The data consists of large static media files stored in object storage and delivered via content delivery networks. Performance and scalability depend heavily on multi-tier caching, geographic replication, and adaptive bitrate streaming, while overall throughput can reach multiple terabits per second in aggregate.

Ad tech and high-throughput analytics systems process enormous volumes of machine-to-machine events, often on the order of billions of events per day. These systems are strongly write-heavy and optimized for ultra-low latency ingestion, frequently targeting p99 latencies below 10 milliseconds. Events are small and usually compressed, flowing through stream-processing pipelines built on distributed log systems such as Kafka or Pulsar. Data is retained briefly for real-time decision-making and stored long-term in analytical databases like ClickHouse or Druid for reporting and historical analysis.

IoT telemetry and sensor systems ingest data from very large numbers of connected devices, often numbering in the millions. Each device sends small payloads at regular intervals, resulting in low bandwidth per device but high total ingestion volume. Traffic is predominantly write-heavy and connectivity is often unreliable, which requires retry mechanisms and backpressure handling. These systems commonly use MQTT or AMQP brokers and store data in time-series databases. Raw telemetry is typically aggregated or downsampled after a short retention period to reduce storage costs.

Search and discovery systems support fuzzy, relevance-based queries for applications such as product search, knowledge bases, and log exploration. They serve both consumer and enterprise users and are heavily query-driven, with far more reads than writes. Data is indexed asynchronously from a primary database into specialized search engines using inverted indexes or vector embeddings. These systems aim to keep query latency under 100 milliseconds and rely on careful shard design, ranking algorithms, and indexing strategies to maintain performance at scale.

API gateway–centric systems sit in front of many internal services and are responsible for request routing, authentication, rate limiting, and protocol translation. They typically handle very high request volumes with relatively small payloads and low per-request compute cost. Latency budgets are tight, but business logic is minimal. These systems must be horizontally scalable, highly available, and resilient to traffic spikes, since they are a single choke point for downstream services.

Transactional OLTP systems power core business operations such as payments, orders, bookings, and account management. They handle moderate to high concurrency with strict correctness requirements and rely on strongly consistent databases. Workloads involve small, frequent reads and writes with ACID guarantees, and failures must be handled carefully to avoid data corruption. Latency targets are usually in the tens of milliseconds, and scaling is often limited by consistency and transaction coordination.

Batch analytics and data warehouse systems are designed to process large volumes of historical data for reporting, business intelligence, and offline analysis. Queries are complex and scan large datasets, but latency requirements are relaxed, often measured in seconds or minutes. Data is ingested in bulk and stored in columnar formats optimized for analytical workloads. These systems trade real-time freshness for cost efficiency and query flexibility.

Event-driven and asynchronous workflow systems coordinate long-running business processes such as order fulfillment, email pipelines, and background processing. They rely on message queues or event buses to decouple producers and consumers. Latency is less critical than reliability, and systems are designed around retries, idempotency, and eventual consistency. Failures are expected and handled through compensating actions rather than immediate rollback.

Caching and acceleration systems exist to reduce load on slower backends and improve response times. They are typically deployed in front of databases or external APIs and store frequently accessed data in memory. Reads vastly outnumber writes, eviction policies play a critical role, and stale data is often tolerated for short periods. These systems improve scalability but introduce complexity around cache invalidation and consistency.

Configuration and feature flag systems manage dynamic configuration values and feature toggles across distributed services. They are read-heavy, require high availability, and must propagate updates quickly but safely. Consistency requirements vary, but partial rollout and controlled exposure are common patterns. These systems are critical for operational control and safe deployments rather than raw performance.

Authentication and identity systems handle user login, token issuance, and permission validation. They must be highly secure, resilient, and low latency, as they sit on the critical path of most user requests. Reads dominate once tokens are issued, but writes occur during registration, login, and credential rotation. Failure modes are severe, so redundancy and careful auditing are essential.

Notification and fan-out systems deliver messages such as emails, push notifications, or SMS to large user populations. They often need to handle bursty traffic triggered by events while respecting rate limits imposed by external providers. Delivery is usually asynchronous and best-effort, with retry mechanisms and dead-letter queues. Latency requirements are flexible, but reliability and observability are crucial.

Recommendation and personalization systems generate ranked content tailored to users, such as product suggestions or feed ordering. They combine offline model training with online inference under tight latency constraints. Reads are frequent and must be fast, while writes occur during model updates or feature ingestion. These systems balance freshness, accuracy, and performance and often rely on approximate algorithms and caching.

Multi-tenant SaaS platforms serve many customers from a shared infrastructure while enforcing strict isolation and fair resource usage. Workloads vary widely across tenants, making noisy-neighbor problems a key concern. Systems must support tenant-aware routing, quotas, and billing, and are often designed with strong observability to understand per-tenant behavior.
